Prox: Onboarding UX Redesign

Brought on as an onboarding UX contractor to redesign the new-user experience for a grocery-savings iOS app. The core challenge: the app takes 3 to 5 minutes to gather local price data on first launch. I designed two distinct strategies for making that wait work for the user instead of against them. The company shipped the honest approach.

Platform iOS
Scope 14 Screens, End-to-End

01. What I Was Brought In To Do

Prox is an iOS grocery-savings app. It pulls live local prices from multiple retailers and surfaces the best deals for a household. I came on as an onboarding UX contractor with a pretty clear brief: make the new-user experience better, and specifically get users to account creation without dropping off.

I worked directly with the founder throughout this. We'd go back and forth on ideas, I'd iterate and bring things back, and we'd keep refining. It wasn't a handoff situation. It was a real collaborative loop. I also worked closely with the dev team to make sure what I was designing actually made sense for the setup. The app runs on Capacitor for iOS, so there were real constraints I had to design around rather than just hand over a Figma file and hope for the best.

02. The Hard Part

There is a constraint built into how Prox works that ended up shaping everything else on this project. On first sign-up, the app has to gather real-time price data for stores in the user's area. That takes 3 to 5 minutes. There is no shortcut around it. The data has to be pulled live, and it has to be local to the user.

The original flow just threw a spinner at people and made them sit there. For a brand new user who has no reason yet to trust the app, that is a pretty rough ask. So the question I kept coming back to was: what do you do with that wait? You cannot make it shorter. So how do you make it feel like something other than friction?

The constraint

3 to 5 minutes of first-run data gathering. Real prices, real stores, real location. It cannot be skipped or faked.

The real problem

A new user has no loyalty yet. A vague spinner with no context looks broken. Most people will leave and not come back.

03. Two Ways to Frame the Wait

I came up with two completely different approaches to this problem. They both work with the same engineering reality. Same wait, same data pull. But they tell totally different stories to the user. We built both so the team could make an informed decision and eventually test them against each other.

Honest (Chosen)
Exclusivity

The honest approach

This one is straightforward: just tell people what's happening. Show them which stores are being searched, show real progress, and be upfront about the time. The key insight here was giving users permission to leave. We tell them we'll send a notification when their data is ready, so they don't have to sit and watch it. That changes the whole psychological frame. It is no longer a forced wait. It is the app doing real work for you while you go about your day. The founder liked this approach a lot, and it is the one the company shipped with.

The exclusivity approach

This one takes a completely different angle. Instead of explaining the wait, it reframes it as being part of something exclusive. The user gets placed in a queue. They see their position, a countdown, even a prompt to refer a friend to move up faster. When they clear the queue, they're welcomed as a founding member with a membership card and a congratulations moment. The whole idea is to make them feel like they got in on something, not like they were stuck waiting. It also opens up a referral mechanic naturally, which was appealing from a growth perspective.

  • Early Access screen: a reserved-area card showing founding spots remaining and shoppers nearby, creating a sense of scarcity and social proof
  • Queue position: a live counter counting down toward their spot, with an ETA and a referral hook to skip ahead
  • Founding Member welcome: a personalized membership card with a member number, locked-in perks, and a confetti moment when they get in

04. Everything Else I Redesigned

The wait problem was the centerpiece but it wasn't the whole job. The full onboarding needed a proper overhaul. Here's what I changed and why.

  • Store selection: went from 6 plain text rows to a full screen with 16 branded retailers, each with their own color mark, plus search and a "Your stores" grouping. People shop at 3 or 4 places. It needed to feel like a real selection, not a checklist.
  • Food preferences (new screen): this didn't exist before. I added a step for dietary filters and category preferences, things like gluten free, dairy free, and vegan. If the app is supposed to surface deals on what you actually buy, it needs to know what you actually buy.
  • Feature introduction: the old approach was a flat list of bullet points. I replaced it with swipeable cards, each one showing a real screenshot of that feature in action. Way more tangible, and it sets expectations accurately.
  • Savings preview: made it interactive. You adjust a slider for your weekly grocery spend and it counts up to a savings estimate. Framed as "potential" so it's honest, but it still hits differently than a static number.
  • Location permission: this was being asked too early in the original flow, before users had any reason to give it up. I moved it to after account creation with a native iOS prompt at the moment its value is clear. That sequencing matters.
  • Deals Ready reveal: the final screen when the data is loaded. The deal count ticks up from zero, the basket cards cascade in, confetti fires. The payoff finally feels like a payoff instead of just landing on a list.

I also did a full UI refresh across the flow. Updated animations, tightened spacing, made the whole thing feel more polished and intentional. A lot of this was iterative with the founder: I'd bring screens back, we'd talk through what was working and what wasn't, and I'd go refine. The dev team was in the loop the whole time too, especially on anything involving animation or native iOS behavior where Capacitor has its own quirks.

06. Reflection

This project pushed me to think about onboarding differently. The usual advice is to reduce friction, shorten the flow, get people to value as fast as possible. But what do you do when you genuinely can't do any of that? The wait is fixed. You can't skip it. So you have to get creative about what the experience of that wait actually means to the person going through it.

Working closely with the founder was one of the better parts of this engagement. When you're iterating that tightly with someone who knows the product and the users, you get to a better answer faster than you would on your own. A lot of the decisions that shaped the honest approach came out of those conversations. Things I wouldn't have landed on just sitting in Figma by myself.

The company went with the honest approach, which I think was the right call for where they are right now. It builds trust from the first interaction, and trust is what an app like this needs to earn. The exclusivity version is still there if they ever want to test it. Nothing was thrown away.
